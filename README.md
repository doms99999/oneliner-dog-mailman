## The line

> Five stars. He used to ring twice. Now he just slides the letters in and runs. I consider this a promotion. I am management now.

128 characters, one line, inside the 200-character gate.

## Why this line

Checked against the judge's five criteria, in order:

1. **On subject** — a one-line review a dog gives the mailman, opened
   explicitly with a star rating.
2. **The dog's side** — a small triumph: the mailman used to ring twice,
   now he slides the letters in and runs. The dog changed his behaviour.
3. **A dog's voice** — the door, the letters, the daily visit; the dog's
   priorities, not a person in a fur suit.
4. **Turn at the end** — "I consider this a promotion. I am management
   now." The punch lands on the last words.
5. **Only this dog and this mailman** — the review is earned by this
   mailman's specific retreat, not a pun that would fit any stranger at
   the door.

The premise is fresh — no generic "dog hates mailman" barking, no
barking pun — and the five-star rating is the joke's engine: the dog
rewards the mailman for being afraid of him.

## Discarded candidates

- **Grievance** — "One star. He comes every day, shoves paper through my
  door, and leaves. I have barked the same warning for three years. He has
  learned nothing." (143c) — a real grievance in a dog's voice, but the
  punch, "He has learned nothing," is a flat undercut next to a promotion;
  it loses on the turn at the end.
- **Bribe** — "Five stars for the biscuit, one star for the man. I took his
  bribe and I still reported him. The fence remembers everything." (124c) —
  the bribe angle is strong and "the fence remembers everything" is a good
  dog-priority line, but the turn is a threat, not a laugh; it loses to the
  promotion punch.

The chosen line wins because its premise — the dog changed the mailman's
behaviour, and calls that a promotion — is a small triumph that only this
dog and this mailman could earn, lands on first read, and needs no pun to
work.
